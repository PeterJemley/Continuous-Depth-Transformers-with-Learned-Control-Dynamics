# What we have done, in plain language

Date: 2026-10-06, updated 2026-10-07 with N5, 2026-10-08 with one added limit, N6 and N6r, 2026-10-09 with N7, 2026-10-10 with N8 and N9, 2026-10-18 with N10, 2026-10-19 with the plan for N11, 2026-10-20 with N11, 2026-10-21 with N11's hardware comparison, corrections from a fresh reader's audit against the notes, and N12, and 2026-10-22 with N13, N14, and N15's withdrawal and N16. Covers the work through N16. The mathematical and geometrical details are in the numbered notes at the end, so the main text can be read without them. Every number comes from a result note in this repository; the notes say which. A critical review of the same work, asking where each guess could have failed and where the method fell short, is in `CRITICAL_REVIEW.md`.

## The starting point: a paper's claims, tested and refuted

The paper proposed a language model with a twist. In the middle of an ordinary transformer, a few layers are replaced by a *continuous* block. Instead of passing a word's representation through a fixed number of steps, the block lets it flow smoothly through "depth", as a ball rolls along a surface whose slopes the model learns.[^1] A small control signal can tilt that surface, and the paper claimed this steered what the model writes, without costing accuracy or speed.

We first tried to reproduce the claims, with plans written and fixed before any run.
- **On a small dataset (WikiText-2), eight times over,** the model was about as accurate as an ordinary transformer, but most of the other claims failed. Steering worked reliably in only 5 of 8 runs. The model was slower, not as fast. A learned scale settled near 0.17, not 0.065. The geometric claim, that the control splits the flow into distinct regions, was not borne out by the paper's own measure.
- **On a dataset about fifty times larger (WikiText-103), trained properly,** the model was clearly worse than an ordinary transformer: by 1.10 nats, in all six runs.[^2]

## Why it failed

The block was trained with a memory-saving shortcut for computing its learning signal, the "adjoint" method. Training the same model with the exact signal instead closed about 95% of the gap: the shortcut alone cost about 0.8 nats.[^3] Trained exactly, the model was only 0.04 nats behind an ordinary transformer at that budget. So the idea was not the main problem; the way it was trained was.

## The real aim: reading the way we hear music

The project began with a conjecture of the author's, not with the paper. When we hear music, we hear several voices at once, each a continuous line, and we follow them all together. Language arrives one word at a time, and that order is right, but a reader still holds many strands at once: characters, arguments, tone, who knows what. The question is whether a model can hold its readings that way, simultaneously and distinctly, and whether continuous dynamics, like the paper's block, help it do so.

From here on, every model was trained with exact gradients, and given two "readings" to perform: predicting the next word, and predicting the word after that.

## The tests, one at a time

Each test was a question the world could answer "no", with the plan, the threshold and the decision rule fixed before any run, and controls built so that the test could fail.

1. **G1. Does the control signal send the flow through regions of different curvature, as the paper suggested?** No. Under one reading the paths through the block bend only 8 to 27% more sharply than under the other. The prediction was at least twice as sharply.[^4] *Refuted.*
2. **G1c. Does it set how strongly the flow pulls nearby states together?** Yes, strongly. Under the farther-ahead reading, the block draws nearby states together 4 to 10 times faster.[^5] *Supported*, and it replicated twice more in later tests.
3. **G1d. Does the model need that stronger pull?** No. Removing over 90% of it during training cost the farther reading nothing extra (−0.002 nats).[^6] *Refuted.* The pattern is real, but not load-bearing.
4. **N1. Then how does the model switch between readings?** A simple shift is enough. Replacing the control's influence on the flow with one fixed vector added after the block did just as well: the differences were 0.001 nats, with intervals inside ±0.01.[^7] *Supported.* Switching between readings needs nothing from the dynamics. Whether the trained model itself works by a shift could not be tested: that claim's check failed, and what the model does looks state-dependent. The state-dependent part is simply not needed.
5. **N2. When both readings must be held at once, does the flow give each its own pace, the way voices differ in tempo?** No. The farther reading's directions settle only 14 to 25% faster than the nearer one's, against a predicted factor of 2.[^8] *Refuted.*

   But N2 noticed something it had not predicted. The two readings lean on partly *different directions* in the model's internal space, overlapping only about 0.6, while two copies of the same reading overlap about 0.99.[^9]
6. **N4. Are the readings kept apart by direction, and does the model need that?** Yes. Forcing both readings to be read through the same 64 directions cost them 0.088 nats more than the same restriction cost a single reading, consistently across five runs.[^10] *Supported.* This is the first test in which a way of holding two readings at once turned out to be needed.

7. **N5. Is N4's cost a competition for directions?** We could not tell. If it were, giving the readout all 256 directions should have removed the cost. It shrank to about a third (+0.030 nats) and was positive in every run, but the runs varied too much for a verdict either way. At narrow widths, training itself became erratic: some runs simply learned worse, which swamped the comparison.[^11] *Inconclusive.* The plan had assumed the runs would vary as little as they did in N4, and they did not.

   N5 did find something it had not predicted, and it held in all 20 models that read two different targets. The two readings always ended up sharing about half of their most important directions (overlap 0.42 to 0.53), whether they started with more in common (0.7) or less (0.2). Training pushed them apart in the first case and together in the second. Two copies of one reading converged instead.[^12]

8. **N6. Does that balance depend on how different the two readings are?** The test could not give a verdict. Its control, two heads reading the same target, had to agree almost perfectly (an overlap of at least 0.95) in 4 of 5 runs, and did so in 3. One run missed by 0.0002. The threshold had been carried over from N4, which measured on almost twice as many passages. With fewer passages, measurement noise keeps even identical readings from scoring as high, and that was not checked in advance.[^13] *Untestable.*

   What the runs showed, though, is what the conjecture predicted. Head B read the word 2, 4 or 8 places ahead. The further ahead, the less the two readings shared: about half at 2 places, under a third at 4, a quarter at 8. That held in the same order in every run. Training started all the pairs at the same point and moved each to its own balance. A new test with its control set correctly did turn this into a verdict: see N6r, next.

9. **N6r. The same test, with its control fixed.** On new runs, measured on fresh passages from the test split, and with the control judged against the measurement's own noise: *supported*, both claims. The further apart the two readings, the less they shared, in the same order in every run: about half for the next word and the word after (0.49), a third for words four apart (0.31), under a quarter for words eight apart (0.23). Two heads reading the same word converged (0.96). The numbers matched N6's to within 0.02.[^14]

10. **N7. Is it "more different", or just "harder"?** Words further ahead are also harder to predict, so N6r could not tell which mattered. N7 added a reading that is the next word exactly, made harder by scrambling 80% of its answers. If sharing tracked how different readings are, this head should have shared almost everything with the clean next-word head. It shared about a third (0.31), as little as the head reading four words ahead (0.30). *Refuted*, both claims.[^15] So the ladder N6r found is real, and it replicated again, but it does not measure how different two readings are, not on its own. The reason suggested at the time was that the measure picks up where a reading's mistakes fall as well as which features it uses. N8 refuted that.

11. **N8. A measure that leaves the mistakes out.** N8 measured the same trained models again, asking which directions change each head's *predictions*, with the right answers left out entirely. The scrambled next-word head still shared only about a third with the clean one (0.31), and still did not converge (agreement ratio 0.33): *refuted*, the two claims about it. The ladder survived: about 0.95, 0.44, 0.25 and 0.19 for words 1, 2, 4 and 8 ahead, *supported*.[^16] The new measure agreed with the old one to within 0.045 on average in each arm. So the old measure was not being fooled by mistakes. The ladder shows which directions each reading's predictions depend on. Why a scrambled copy of the next-word reading ends up using different directions is still open. One possibility is that it learned a weaker, different version of the reading. Another is that this kind of measure cannot recognise a noisy copy as the same reading at all. N8 did not check that. N9 did.

12. **N9. Can the measure recognise a noisy copy?** N9 built, from each clean next-word head, the perfect scrambled-answer version of it: a head that predicts exactly 20% of what the clean head predicts plus 80% word frequencies, with no training. The measure called this copy the same reading as the clean head (about 0.98): *supported*. The scrambled head N7 actually trained was nowhere near the copy (about 0.32): *refuted*.[^17] So the measure is not the problem. Trained on scrambled answers, the head learned a different and weaker reading of the context, not a noisy version of the right one. It still predicts almost the same words as the perfect copy overall, because both are mostly word frequencies, but the part that depends on the passage differs.

13. **N10. How much scrambling does it take, and can a small "payload" protect a reading?** New runs scrambled 20%, 50% or 80% of the second head's answers. The more scrambling, the further the head drifted from its perfect noisy copy, in that order in every run: *supported*. At 20% the head predicted almost exactly what the perfect copy would (within 0.04 nats), yet the directions it read had already drifted a little, just short of the bar for "the same" (0.81 against 0.85): *intermediate*. Three more arms at 80% scrambling (15 runs) each got a small payload: matching the perfect copy on 64 passages, pinning the head's directions to the clean head's, or clean answers on the same 64 passages. The main payload test gave no verdict: the matching payload failed the check that it had taken hold. The likely reason is that it was too weak, which was my design error; N11 later found that the check's target may also be harder to reach than it looked. The check written for exactly this case caught it. Of the other two, pinning the directions made the head *look* closer to the copy but barely improved what it predicted, and it made the clean head worse. Clean answers on 64 passages backfired: the network memorised those passages.[^18]

14. **N11. A stronger payload, judged against a yardstick it cannot move.** N11 tried the matching payload again at 8, 32 and 128 times N10's weight, in a changed form. It now trained only the second head's own readout, so that its learning signal could not reach the clean head; the cost to the clean head was then measured at almost nothing. It was measured against the perfect copy from a run with no payload, which no payload can change. It still failed its own check. By the end of training it had brought the second head only to 59% of the unprotected head's distance from the copy on its passages, against at most 50% required. So the main claims got no verdict: *untestable*, again. A trial run beforehand had looked fine, but it stopped at a tenth of training, and I misread a number that was rising as one that was holding: my error, a second time. Stronger payloads did help more, in every run: *supported*, though the gains flattened (40%, 45% and 47% of the gap closed). A later check on the runs the first, interrupted session had finished showed that the last of those gains, from 32 to 128 times, was no bigger than the difference the kind of GPU itself makes. What the runs showed is still striking, though these are secondary measures and decide nothing. The scrambling costs the second head about 1.5 nats, and a perfect noisy copy would still pay about 1 nat of that, which no payload can recover. Of the rest, the half nat that an ideal reading of the scrambled answers would avoid, the matching payload won back nearly half on passages it never saw, at almost no cost to the clean head. Pinning the directions won back a fifth, and made the clean head worse. The matching payload hardly moved the directions the head reads; the pin moved them most of the way back. What a head predicts and the directions it reads came apart.[^19]

15. **N12. Is the scrambled head held back by the shape of its readout?** Since N7 a head trained on scrambled answers had read different directions from the clean head, and N9 had found it was not even the perfect noisy copy. N12 tested one explanation. The best possible answer to a scrambled question is a blend: one fifth the true prediction, four fifths word frequencies. A standard readout, one softmax over a few directions, cannot produce a blend. So N12 gave the second head a readout that *can*, by building the blend into its output, and trained it on the same scrambled answers. It closed 71% of the gap to the perfect copy: *supported*. A control given the clean answer at exactly the positions the scrambling had left alone, and nothing elsewhere, closed 80%, so too few useful answers was not, on its own, the cause: *supported*. A standard readout fitted directly to the copy, as well as our fitting could manage, closed only 62%, so no standard readout we could find reaches the copy: *supported*, in that modest sense. The blended head's directions moved most of the way to the copy's, from 0.31 to 0.83, but stopped just under the 0.85 bar in every run: *intermediate*, the same near miss as N10's head at 20% scrambling. So the explanation survived its tests of function and fell just short on geometry. The numbers also divide the drift: the readout's shape accounts for at most two fifths of the gap, and training through that shape on scrambled answers for the rest. Two secondary measures: the standard scrambled head cost the clean head 0.04 nats, and the blended head cost it little or nothing (an interval from −0.02 to +0.01).[^20]
16. **N13. What does the blended readout do: represent the blend, or learn differently?** N12 had left two candidate reasons for the blended head's success: its readout can produce the blend, and its training discounts answers it judges to be scrambled. A line of calculus shows the two are not separate in training: a blended readout trains its inner prediction exactly as a standard readout would if each answer counted by the head's own estimate that the answer is genuine. N13 tested three things. First, that the code does what the calculus says: a standard readout trained with exactly that discount matched the blended head's inner prediction to within 0.005 nats in every run, against an allowance of 0.03: *supported*. Second, that the blend's shape does nothing on its own: placing the standard scrambled head's prediction into the blend after training doubled its distance from the perfect copy: *supported*. Third, that the discount works by telling genuine answers from scrambled ones, not merely by making the second head pull less on the shared network: a head whose every answer counted a fifth, about the same average pull without the discrimination, gained nothing (+0.05 nats, where a quarter of the gain would have been −0.27): *supported*. So the blended readout's advantage is in what it learns. By the end of training its discount counted a genuine answer 5.6 times a scrambled one, and a standard readout can hold what that training aims at. Two secondary findings. The constant fifth removed the second head's cost to the clean head, within the measure's noise, as the blended head had, so on this evidence the cost tracks how hard the second head pulls rather than how scrambled its answers are; though one control, clean answers on a fifth of the positions, cost more than the fifth-weight scrambled head did, which is not understood. And a repeat of the blended head's training was identical to the first to the last bit. One error in the stated mechanism was caught before the run: the discount cannot tell genuine from scrambled answers at the start, since both are ordinary words in ordinary proportions; it learns to, from context.[^21]
17. **N14. Do scrambled answers leave a trace in the directions that accuracy does not show?** N12 had found the blended head and a control given only the genuine answers nearly as accurate as each other, yet reading the clean head's directions at 0.81 and 0.90, and had conjectured that scrambled answers leave a trace in the directions beyond their cost to accuracy. N14 built a ladder of heads trained on fewer and fewer genuine answers (20, 10, 5, 2.5 and 1.25% of positions) and no scrambled ones, which gives, for any accuracy, the directions a head of that accuracy reads; and it trained heads with 10, 20 and 40% scrambled answers on top of the fixed 20% genuine ones, to compare with the ladder at matched accuracy. The scrambled-answer heads sat on the ladder: at matched accuracy their directions were within about 0.02 of a genuine-answer head's (0.01 at the largest dose), with an uncertainty of about ±0.04, so the primary claim is *inconclusive* by its rule, and the numbers favour the rival, that geometry follows accuracy. The ladder also says where the 0.85 bar lies: a head reaches it when its accuracy is within about a quarter of a nat of the clean head's, and the blended head of N12 was a third of a nat away, so, on this evidence, its near miss has an explanation that needs no trace. The trace's growth with the dose was *intermediate*: the smallest dose carried about half of it, and the trace and the loss of accuracy both grew with the share of the training signal that the scrambled answers kept. The third claim, that the blended readout's reweighting of the genuine answers does not move the directions by itself, was *refuted* as stated: a head given that reweighting and no scrambled answers lost 0.12 of agreement. But its accuracy collapsed too, to near that of a head with 1.25% genuine answers, while its directions stayed those of a 5% head: better than its accuracy predicts, not worse. The calibration had shown that this control would be read that way; the rule stood as approved, and the better reading is reported beside it as exploratory. So the conjecture did not survive, and what stands in its place, within the test's noise, is simpler: along the axis of scrambled answers, what a head reads follows how well it predicts.[^22]
18. **N15 and N16. Is the blended head held back by the scrambled answers it still counts, or by who does the counting?** The discount that makes the blended head work (N13) counts each answer by the second head's own estimate that the answer is genuine. Trained that way, the second head ends a sixth of a nat short of a head given only the genuine answers. N15 conjectured that the shortfall was the scrambled answers' remaining weight, and tried sharper discounts that would cut it. Its full-length calibration, before any real run, found that the sharper discounts did not train at all, while a discount computed by the *clean* head instead recovered the whole shortfall and left most of the scrambled weight in place. The author withdrew N15 and preregistered N16 from that observation. N16's conjecture: the shortfall comes from the second head judging itself. A genuine answer it predicts poorly gets little weight, so it never learns it. A judge that does not share its errors has no such blind spot. On five new runs, the clean-head-judged discount recovered all of the shortfall and a little more (108%, where at least 75% was needed): *supported*. It did so while removing only a tenth of the scrambled answers' share of the weight (less than half was needed): *supported*. On genuine answers alone, the clean head's judgement cost 0.45 nats less than the second head's own (at least 0.30 was needed): *supported*. Whether the recovered head's directions follow its accuracy along N14's ladder was *inconclusive*, at the limit the design had stated, with the point estimate inside the margin. Two cautions. The clean head is a better predictor as well as an independent one, and the design does not say which of the two matters; the next test is built to. And a comparison of all four combinations (exploratory) showed the scrambled answers *helping* the reweighted head under either judge, more when it judges itself, so on this evidence they are not only a cost to be removed. A clean head is something a real noisy problem does not have, so this judge is a diagnostic, not a method.[^23]

## What we now understand

- **The voices are separated by direction, not by tempo.** When one internal state must serve two readings, the readings occupy partly different directions within it, and squeezing them into the same directions costs accuracy. They do not get different timescales in the continuous block.
- **Two related voices share about half their space, and training finds that balance.** The next word and the word after settle at roughly half-overlap from either side (N5), and the half-overlap held again on new runs (N6r).
- **Readings further ahead depend on different directions (N6r, N7, N8).** The overlap falls from a half to a third to a quarter as the second reading looks further ahead. That replicated three times, and it held under a second measure that leaves the right answers out (N8). So it is about what the readings' predictions depend on, not about their mistakes. But a reading that is the same as the next word, only scrambled, shares nearly as little. So "the more different the voices, the more separate their directions", which this summary stated as a rule after N6r, did not survive its test. N9 ruled out the measure as the cause: it recognises a perfect noisy copy, and the scrambled head is not one. That head learned a different, weaker reading. N12 then divided the reason: a standard readout cannot represent the blend a scrambled answer calls for, which accounts for at most two fifths of the drift, and training through that shape on scrambled answers accounts for the rest. A readout that can represent the blend keeps most of the function (71% of the gap) and most of the directions (0.83). N16 found where the rest of the shortfall lies, in the discount's judge: counted by the clean head's judgement instead of its own, the same head did as well as a head given only the genuine answers, with most of the scrambled answers still counting. Whether the clean head helps by being independent of the second head or by being the better predictor is open.
- **Why sharing costs is not yet settled.** N5 could not tell competition for directions from a clash of learning signals; some cost may remain even when nothing is restricted.
- **What a head predicts and the directions it reads can be restored separately** (N10, N11). Anchoring the predictions restored much of what a scrambled head predicts without restoring its directions. Pinning the directions did the reverse. This comes from secondary measures, not a tested claim: the claim meant to test it got no verdict.
- **The continuous dynamics are not where the readings live,** at this scale. Switching readings needs only a shift (N1). Holding them at once relies on geometry, the arrangement of directions, rather than on the flow's dynamics (N2, N4).
- **This matches the music analogy better than the original picture did.** Listeners follow several voices more easily when the voices differ in timbre, the quality that tells instruments apart (the evidence is in `CONJECTURE.md`). Here, a direction plays the part of timbre.
- **Refutations taught as much as successes.** Each "no" ruled out a mechanism and pointed to the next question. None was rescued after the fact. Checks before the real run caught problems in six tests (G1, N2, N4, N7, N8, N9): on untrained models in G1, N2 and N8, where in G1 the original measure would have called an untrained model a success; by a loss bound in N7; by a reread of the plan in N4; and in N9 a caution that turned out not to be needed. They missed N6's threshold, which was not re-checked for a smaller measurement, and the short full-size checks before N10 and N11 missed that their payloads would not take hold.

## Limits

- Small models (about 30 million parameters, or 23 to 43 million with the two-reading heads), one dataset, 10% of the full training budget, and one learning rate.
- Only two readings, and closely related ones: the next word and the word after.
- N4 restricted the model's final readout, not the continuous block, and with only 64 of 256 directions. That is a tight squeeze.
- How much the results vary from run to run depends on the readout width. The measured cost varied about 3 to 24 times more between runs at N5's widths than at N4's width of 64 (standard deviations 0.022 to 0.194, against 0.008).[^11] So a plan's estimate of how sensitive a test will be cannot be carried from one width to another, and this is why N5 was inconclusive.

## What comes next

The candidates, in order:
- **Independent judge or better judge?** N16's clean-head judge is both. Add a third head trained exactly as the self-judging second head is, and let it judge the second head's answers, as co-teaching lets each network judge its peer's: the judge is then no more accurate than the self-judging head, but the second head no longer judges itself. If it recovers the shortfall, judging oneself is the problem; if not, the judge's accuracy is.
- **A floor on the discount,** so that no genuine answer counts for less than a minimum: whether the self-judging head's blind spot can be removed without a second head.
- **What the scrambled answers carry under a clean judge** (exploratory). With the clean head judging, the second head did slightly better than a head given only the genuine answers, and the extra came with the scrambled answers. A judge applied to the scrambled positions only, and to the genuine ones only, would separate the two roles.
- **Why the cost to the clean head is not monotone in the second head's genuine answers** (exploratory). A second head with a fifth of the genuine answers costs the clean head more than one with all of them or with almost none, and scrambled answers lower the cost. N13's reading, that the cost tracks how hard the second head pulls, does not cover this.
- **Return to N10's question with blended readouts.** N10 and N11 asked whether anchoring what a head predicts preserves its reading better than pinning its directions, and both payloads failed their checks. Both were aimed at the blend, which N12's fits show a standard readout does not reach, though N12 did not test whether that is why the checks failed. With a blended readout the target is reachable, which removes at least that obstacle.
- **Does the cost of sharing grow along the ladder?** Repeat N4's forced-sharing test at 2, 4 and 8 words ahead. This measures what sharing *costs*, which N7 does not touch.
- **Does the cost to the clean head grow with how hard the second head pulls?** A dose of the constant discount (a twentieth, a fifth, a half, all) on the standard scrambled head. N13's secondary findings say the cost tracks the pull, and its control with clean answers on a fifth of the positions says the content matters too.
- **Readings that differ in kind, not only in distance,** such as a topic label.
- **Settle N5's main question with enough runs:** about ten runs at full width would tell a cost of 0.03 from none.
- **More voices at once,** three or four readings, at the width where training is consistent.
- **Move the restriction into the continuous block,** which would tie the results back to the original conjecture.

## Notes

[^1]: **The continuous block.** A word's representation h, a list of 256 numbers, evolves through "depth" τ from 0 to 1 by the differential equation dh/dτ = α·F(h, τ, u), where F is a small learned network, α a learned scale and u the control signal. The model solves it in 4 small steps (Euler's method). The paper calls the model "Model A"; it is the paper's `HybridODETransformer`. Sources: `../../MATH_EXPLAINER.md`, `../../model.py`.

[^2]: **Nats and intervals.** Accuracy is measured as loss, the average of −ln p over every word, where p is the probability the model gave the word that actually came next. A difference of 0.05 nats, the margin most tests used (the WikiText-103 study used 0.02), is about a 5% change in how uncertain the model is (e^0.05 ≈ 1.05). Intervals are t-intervals across independent runs (5 to 8 seeds), paired when the runs share starting conditions: 95%, or 97.5% where a test had two interval rules to correct for (N1, N5, N8, N10, N11, N12, N13). WikiText-103: Model A minus baseline +1.10 nats, 95% interval [+0.97, +1.23], 6 seeds, Model A worse in all 6. Sources: `../wikitext2-replication/RESULTS.md`, `../wikitext103-training/RESULTS.md`.

[^3]: **Adjoint against exact gradients.** The adjoint method recomputes the flow backwards to save memory, instead of storing it, so its learning signal is an approximation. Cost of training with it rather than exactly: +0.79 nats, 95% interval [+0.66, +0.92], about 95% of the 0.83-nat gap at 10% of the budget. A direct comparison of the two gradients themselves (T0) was untestable under its own rules, so how the approximation goes wrong is not established. Sources: `../too-linear-conjecture/T1_NOTE.md`, `../too-linear-conjecture/T0_NOTE.md`.

[^4]: **Curvature.** For a path h(τ) with velocity v = dh/dτ and acceleration a = d²h/dτ², curvature is κ = √(|v|²|a|² − (v·a)²) / |v|³, how sharply the path turns. G1 compared mean curvature under the two control values: ratios 1.08 to 1.27 in five models; at least 2 was required in 4 of 5. Amendment A1 replaced an effect-size measure (Cohen's d) with this ratio before any real run, because untrained models gave d up to 6.0 and would have passed. Source: `G1_NOTE.md`.

[^5]: **Contraction.** The block's flow has a Jacobian J = ∂F/∂h, which says how a small change in the state changes its velocity. Its trace, the divergence of the flow, measures whether nearby states spread apart (positive) or are drawn together (negative). Contraction is minus the divergence, −tr(J), per token. It is estimated without forming J, by Hutchinson's method: tr(J) ≈ average of zᵀJz over random vectors z of ±1s, with Jz computed by forward-mode differentiation. Ratios (u = −1 over u = +1): 4.1 to 10.2 in five new models; shuffled-control models 0.997 to 1.031. Source: `G1C_NOTE.md`.

[^6]: **Capping contraction.** During training, the loss was given an extra term λ·max(0, c − 2)², where c is the contraction estimate on farther-reading sequences, so contraction above 2 per token was penalised. The capped models' contraction was 0.37–0.65, against 7.4–8.7 in uncapped models trained alongside them. The measure D was the change in the farther reading's loss minus the change in the next-word loss: −0.002, 95% interval [−0.007, +0.002]. Source: `G1D_NOTE.md`.

[^7]: **An offset.** In the "post" model, u never enters F. The block's output is h(1) + e(u), where e(u) is a learned vector, one per control value. Differences from the full model: +0.0010 and +0.0008 nats for the two readings, 97.5% intervals within [−0.008, +0.010]. Source: `N1_NOTE.md`.

[^8]: **Timescales within a reading's directions.** For each reading k, its sensitive directions were found first (note 9). Contraction within them was then measured as c_k = −E[zᵀJz]/r, with probes z = U_k ε confined to that reading's r = 16 directions. A higher rate means the flow forgets differences in those directions faster: a shorter timescale. Ratios (farther over nearer): 1.14 to 1.25; at least 2 was required. Source: `N2_NOTE.md`.

[^9]: **A reading's directions, and how much two readings share.** For each reading, the gradient g of its loss with respect to the block's output is collected for every word, and the matrix G = Σ g gᵀ is formed. Its top 16 eigenvectors, the columns of U, span the directions the reading is most sensitive to. The overlap of two readings is ‖U_Aᵀ U_B‖²_F / 16, the mean squared cosine of the principal angles between the two subspaces. It is 1 for identical subspaces, and about 16/256 ≈ 0.06 for random ones. N2: 0.57 to 0.63 for two readings, 0.98 to 0.99 for two copies of one. Source: `N2_NOTE.md`.

[^10]: **The shared-directions test.** Each reading's prediction is made through a projection: logits_k = W_k P_k z, where z is the final state after normalisation (256 numbers), P_k a 64 × 256 matrix and W_k maps to the vocabulary. "Separate" lets P_A and P_B differ; "tied" forces P_A = P_B. P_B started as a copy of P_A, so the two versions were identical until training. The measure is a difference of differences, which removes the general cost of a narrow readout: D = (J_dual-tied − J_dual-sep) − (J_same-tied − J_same-sep), with J the sum of the two readings' losses. D = +0.088, 95% interval [+0.078, +0.097]. Overlap at the block's output: 0.46–0.49 for two readings, against 0.95–0.97 for two copies of one; untrained, 0.455–0.501. Sources: `N4_NOTE.md`, `results/n4_exploratory/untrained_overlap.json`.

[^11]: **Why the full width decides.** At width 256, P is a square matrix, invertible from the start. Then W_k P can be any matrix W_k could be on its own, so tied and separate readouts can compute exactly the same things. Any remaining cost of tying must come from training, not from a lack of directions. N5's primary test: D at width 256, supported if its 97.5% interval lies below 0.02, refuted if it lies above 0.05. Result: D(256) = +0.030, 97.5% interval [−0.004, +0.065], positive in all 5 seeds; inconclusive. N5b, D(32) − D(128) = −0.063, interval [−0.32, +0.20]; inconclusive. The standard deviation of D across seeds was 0.022 at width 256, 0.060 at 128 and 0.194 at 32, against about 0.008 at 64 in N4. Sources: `N5_PREREGISTRATION.md`, `N5_NOTE.md`.
